## The Power of Small Habits (`habits`, 262 words, 4 paragraphs)

- Class profile: 高一普通班，程度落差大，約三分之一學生閱讀長文會放棄
- Objectives: I can explain why small habits can matter more than big efforts.; I can compare the two students' ways of studying.; I can use because and for example to give reasons and examples.
- Settings: levels=['basic', 'standard'], minutes=30, student_tasks=['理解內容', '比較／分類', '寫作'], answer_formats=['簡答題', '表格整理', '填空題'], generation=['加入暖身活動', '顯示學習目標', '提供引導問題', '提供字詞表', '提供句型框架']

> Many people believe that changing their lives requires a dramatic decision, such as quitting a job or moving to a new city. However, research on human behavior suggests that small, repeated actions often matter more than big moments. A habit is a behavior that we perform so often that it becomes automatic. Because habits save mental energy, the brain is happy to keep them, whether they help us or hurt us.

> Consider two students who want to improve their English. The first student studies for five hours the night before every exam and then stops. The second student reads for fifteen minutes every evening. After one year, the second student has spent more than ninety hours reading, and the practice has become part of her daily routine. Small efforts, repeated over time, add up in a way that occasional bursts of effort rarely do.

> Building a good habit is easier when the behavior is simple and connected to something we already do. For example, a person who wants to drink more water might keep a glass next to the coffee machine. Each morning, making coffee becomes a reminder to drink. Experts call this "habit stacking," because the new habit is placed on top of an old one.

> Of course, small habits do not create instant results, and this is why many people give up. The changes are often invisible at first. Yet, like a seed growing under the soil, progress is happening even when we cannot see it. Patience, not talent, is usually what separates people who succeed from those who quit.

> Every Friday evening, Kai helps his grandmother sell scallion pancakes at a small night market stall. The stall is only two meters wide, but the line of customers is often the longest on the street.

> At first, Kai thought the job would be easy. He soon learned that his grandmother does many things at once. She mixes the dough, watches the hot pan, takes orders, and still remembers the names of her regular customers. When Kai tried to do just two of these jobs, he burned three pancakes in ten minutes.

> One night, Kai asked her how she could stay so calm. She smiled and said that she was not calm at all when she started forty years ago. "I made every mistake you can imagine," she told him. Now, when Kai burns a pancake, he eats it himself and tries again.

## Cooling the Urban Heat Island (`heat`, 326 words, 6 paragraphs)

- Class profile: 高二普通班自然組，程度中上，能閱讀較長的說明文，但寫作表達較弱
- Objectives: I can explain the causes of the urban heat island and ways to reduce it.; I can evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of each solution.; I can use cause-and-effect words to write an explanation.
- Settings: levels=['standard', 'advanced'], minutes=40, student_tasks=['理解內容', '整理／分析資料', '評估／提出理由', '寫作'], answer_formats=['簡答題', '表格整理', '圖像組織', '段落寫作'], generation=['加入暖身活動', '顯示學習目標', '提供引導問題', '顯示作答示例', '提供句型框架']

> On a summer afternoon, the center of a large city can be several degrees warmer than the farmland only a few kilometers away. Scientists call this difference the urban heat island effect. It is not simply a matter of comfort: higher temperatures increase electricity use, worsen air pollution, and put elderly residents at serious risk during heat waves.

> The causes are largely built into the city itself. Dark asphalt roads and roofs absorb sunlight during the day and release the stored heat slowly at night, so the city never fully cools down. Tall buildings trap warm air and block the wind that might otherwise carry it away. In addition, air conditioners, cars, and factories pump waste heat directly into the streets.

> Vegetation is one of the most effective natural defenses. Trees provide shade, and their leaves release water vapor, a process that cools the surrounding air in much the same way that sweat cools the human body. Studies of several Asian cities have found that streets lined with mature trees can be noticeably cooler at midday than nearby streets without them.

> Some cities are also experimenting with "cool" materials. Roofs painted white or light gray reflect a large share of incoming sunlight instead of absorbing it. Certain new pavements allow rainwater to soak in and later evaporate, which lowers the surface temperature. These solutions are not perfect; reflective surfaces can create glare, and special pavements cost more to maintain.

> Critics argue that such projects mainly benefit wealthier neighborhoods, which already tend to have more parks and trees. For this reason, planners in several cities now map surface temperatures block by block and direct new trees and cool roofs to the hottest, often poorer, districts first.

> In the end, no single technology will solve the problem. A cooler city is likely to be the result of many modest choices, from the color of a roof to the location of a single tree, made consistently over many years.
