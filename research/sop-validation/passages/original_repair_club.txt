When the zipper on Lin Yu-chen's backpack broke last spring, her first thought was to buy a new one. The bag was only a year old, but the nearest repair shop had closed, and a new backpack cost less than she expected. On her way to the store, however, she passed the school's recycling corner and stopped. Behind the bins lay a pile of umbrellas, desk lamps, and headphones, most of them thrown away because of one small broken part.

That pile bothered her for days. In her civics class she had learned that Taiwan recycles more than half of its household waste, a number many countries admire. Yet recycling still uses energy, and it does nothing to reduce how much people buy. If a lamp only needs a new switch, she wondered, why should the whole lamp be melted down? The real problem, she decided, was not that students were careless. It was that nobody had ever shown them how to fix things.

So Yu-chen wrote a short proposal and took it to her homeroom teacher. Her plan was simple: every Wednesday after school, students could bring broken items to an empty classroom, and volunteers would repair them together. The teacher liked the idea but warned her that enthusiasm fades quickly. To keep the club alive, Yu-chen invited a retired electrician from the neighborhood and a parent who ran a tailoring shop to act as coaches.

The first meeting was almost a failure. Only four students came, and two of them left when they realized they had to do the repairs themselves. Still, by the end of the afternoon a pair of headphones was working again, and its owner posted a video online. The following week, nineteen students were waiting at the door.

A year later, the Repair Club has fixed more than three hundred items. Yu-chen admits that this number is tiny compared with what the school throws away. But she believes the club changes something more important than the amount of trash. "Once you have opened a machine and understood it," she says, "you stop seeing it as something to throw away."
